1What a mortgage is

A mortgage is a loan to buy a home. You pay part of the price up front (the down payment) and borrow the rest. The home backs the loan: if you can't repay, the lender can sell it to recover what it's owed. Each payment covers interest, the lender's fee for lending, and principal, the amount you actually borrowed.

2Term vs amortization

Amortization is how long it would take to pay the mortgage off completely, commonly 25 years. Term is how long your current deal lasts, commonly 5 years. When a term ends you renew, and the lender offers a new rate for the next term. That's why one amortization usually means several terms.

3Where your payment goes

Your payment stays the same, but what's inside it changes. Interest is charged on what you still owe, so early payments are mostly interest. As the balance shrinks, more of each payment pays down principal.

4What equity is

Equity is the part of your home you own: what it's worth minus what you still owe. It starts as your down payment and grows as you pay the mortgage down. If the home's value rises, your equity grows faster, and if it falls, equity shrinks. These bars assume the value stays the same.

5Insured vs uninsured mortgages

If you put down less than 20% of the purchase price, your mortgage is called high-ratio and must be insured. The insurance, called mortgage default insurance, is provided by CMHC, Sagen or Canada Guaranty. It protects the lender if the loan isn't repaid, not you. In exchange for that protection, lenders will lend to buyers with smaller down payments, and often at lower interest rates.

Insured (under 20% down)

  • You pay a one-time insurance premium, based on your down payment.
  • The premium is usually added to your mortgage and repaid with interest. In some provinces, sales tax on the premium is due in cash at closing.
  • The home must cost less than $1.5 million.
  • Usually a 25-year maximum amortization. Some buyers, such as first-time buyers and buyers of new builds, can choose 30 years for a 0.20% higher premium.
  • Often a lower interest rate than an uninsured mortgage.
  • You can't cancel the insurance. It stays with the loan.

Uninsured (20% or more down)

  • No insurance premium.
  • Also called a conventional mortgage.
  • Available at any price. For homes of $1.5 million or more, 20% down is required anyway.
  • Your interest rate may be slightly higher than an insured rate, but you avoid the premium.

Example: 10% down on a $500,000 home means a $450,000 mortgage and a 3.10% premium of $13,950, so you'd borrow $463,950. The monthly payment calculator does this for you when your down payment is under 20%.

Minimum down payment

You need at least 5% of the first $500,000 of the price, plus 10% of the part between $500,000 and $1.5 million. At $1.5 million and above you need 20%. If your down payment comes from borrowed money, the premium is higher.

Premiums and rules are based on CMHC's published rates as reported in 2026 guides, and Sagen and Canada Guaranty price similarly. Rules change, so confirm the details with your lender or mortgage professional.

6How much can you borrow? GDS and TDS

When you apply, a lender checks two ratios to decide how much you can borrow. Both compare your monthly costs to your gross monthly income, which is your income before tax.

  • GDS (gross debt service) counts housing costs only: your mortgage payment, property tax, heating, and half of any condo fees.
  • TDS (total debt service) counts those housing costs plus all your other monthly debt payments, such as car loans, credit cards, lines of credit and student loans.

For insured mortgages, CMHC's maximums are 39% for GDS and 44% for TDS, and the tighter of the two sets your limit. The mortgage payment is tested at a higher "stress-test" rate than you'd actually pay: the greater of your rate plus 2% or 5.25%. That way you're only approved for a payment you could still handle if rates rose.

GDS = (mortgage payment + property tax + heating + 50% of condo fees) ÷ gross monthly income TDS = (those housing costs + other monthly debt payments) ÷ gross monthly income Gross monthly income = yearly income before tax ÷ 12

7Do the math yourself

  1. Mortgage amount = home price − down payment. If you put down less than 20%, add the insurance premium too.
  2. Rate per month (call it i). Fixed: (1 + annual rate ÷ 2)^(1/6) − 1, because Canadian fixed rates compound twice a year. Variable: annual rate ÷ 12, since variable rates usually compound monthly.
  3. Number of payments (n) = years × 12
  4. Monthly payment = mortgage × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^(−n))
  5. Each month: interest = balance × i. Principal = payment − interest. New balance = balance − principal. Then repeat.
  6. Equity = what the home is worth − what you still owe

The whole formula in one line (steps 2 to 4 combined):

Fixed rate: Payment = P × [(1 + r÷2)^(1÷6) − 1] ÷ [1 − (1 + r÷2)^(−2 × y)] Variable rate: Payment = P × (r÷12) ÷ [1 − (1 + r÷12)^(−12 × y)]

P = mortgage amount (plus any insurance premium) · r = yearly rate as a decimal (4.84% is 0.0484) · y = amortization in years
