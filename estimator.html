    Insured vs uninsured mortgages

    If you put down less than 20% of the purchase price, your mortgage is called high-ratio and must be insured. The insurance, called mortgage default insurance, is provided by CMHC, Sagen or Canada Guaranty. It protects the lender if the loan isn't repaid, not you. In exchange for that protection, lenders will lend to buyers with smaller down payments, and often at lower interest rates.

    Insured (under 20% down)

    • You pay a one-time insurance premium, based on your down payment.
    • The premium is usually added to your mortgage and repaid with interest. In some provinces, sales tax on the premium is due in cash at closing.
    • The home must cost less than $1.5 million.
    • Usually a 25-year maximum amortization. Some buyers, such as first-time buyers and buyers of new builds, can choose 30 years for a 0.20% higher premium.
    • Often a lower interest rate than an uninsured mortgage.
    • You can't cancel the insurance. It stays with the loan.

    Uninsured (20% or more down)

    • No insurance premium.
    • Also called a conventional mortgage.
    • Available at any price. For homes of $1.5 million or more, 20% down is required anyway.
    • Your interest rate may be slightly higher than an insured rate, but you avoid the premium.

    Example: 10% down on a $500,000 home means a $450,000 mortgage and a 3.10% premium of $13,950, so you'd borrow $463,950. The estimator above does this for you when your down payment is under 20%.

    Minimum down payment

    You need at least 5% of the first $500,000 of the price, plus 10% of the part between $500,000 and $1.5 million. At $1.5 million and above you need 20%. If your down payment comes from borrowed money, the premium is higher.

    Premiums and rules are based on CMHC's published rates as reported in 2026 guides, and Sagen and Canada Guaranty price similarly. Rules change, so confirm the details with your lender or mortgage professional.
